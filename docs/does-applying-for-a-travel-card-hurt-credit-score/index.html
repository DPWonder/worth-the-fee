How much does applying for a travel card affect your credit score, and for how long?

Facts checked November 20, 20264 min readHow we check facts

This page contains a referral link. We earn points or cash if you sign up through it. How we earn.

The short answer

For most people, one credit card application lowers a FICO score by fewer than five points, according to FICO. The hard inquiry counts in FICO scores for 12 months and stays on your credit report for two years, and the new account also lowers the average age of your accounts.

The numbers

Item FICO VantageScore
Typical drop from one hard inquiry Less than five points Five to 10 points
How long the inquiry is counted in the score 12 months Up to 24 months
How long the effect usually lasts A few months A few months
How long the inquiry stays on the report Two years Two years

The FICO figures come from myFICO. The VantageScore figures come from Experian. FICO adds that the drop can be larger for people with a short credit history.

Sources disagree on one point: a credit expert quoted by CNBC Select says both scoring models ignore inquiries after one year, while Experian says VantageScore can count them for 24 months.

Three things an application changes

1. A hard inquiry. Applying for a card puts a hard inquiry on your report whether or not you are approved. FICO says inquiries play "a minor part in only 10%" of the score, the part called new credit.

2. A new account. If approved, the new card lowers the average age of your accounts. Length of credit history is 15% of a FICO score. No source read gives a point figure for this, and it depends on how many accounts you have and how old they are.

3. More available credit. How much of your available credit you use is part of the amounts-owed category, which is 30% of a FICO score. A new credit line can lower that share if your balances stay the same. It raises it if you run up the new card.

FICO score category Weight Affected by a new card?
Payment history 35% Only by how you pay afterward
Amounts owed 30% Yes, either way
Length of credit history 15% Yes, lower average age
New credit 10% Yes, the inquiry and new account
Credit mix 10% Usually little

Card applications are not grouped

For mortgages, auto loans and student loans, several inquiries in a short window are counted as one. Experian says that does not apply to credit cards: each card application is counted separately, however close together they are.

An older Experian article says several inquiries close together can have a larger effect, and suggests waiting six months to a year between applications.

Other effects that are not about the score

  • Chase generally declines applicants who opened five or more personal cards in 24 months. Each new card, from any bank, counts toward that. See the 5/24 rule.
  • Payment history is the largest part of the score at 35%. It depends on paying on time once you have the card.

Who should skip a new application for now

  • Anyone applying for a mortgage, car loan or other large loan in the next several months. A few points can matter at a rate cutoff. Experian's six-to-twelve-month spacing is a cautious guide.
  • Anyone with a thin or short credit history. FICO says the effect of an inquiry is larger for them.
  • Anyone with several recent card applications.
  • Anyone who expects to carry a balance on the new card. Amounts owed is 30% of a FICO score against 10% for new credit, and interest can outweigh a welcome offer.

A small score effect says nothing about whether a card is worth its fee. For that, see Amex Platinum for an occasional flyer or Sapphire Preferred on one trip a year.

Quick answers

How many points does a credit card application cost? FICO says less than five points for most people. Experian puts the typical VantageScore drop at five to 10 points.

How long does a hard inquiry stay on my credit report? Two years. FICO scores count it for only the first 12 months.

Does being denied hurt more than being approved? The inquiry is the same either way. A denial adds no new account, so it does not change the average age of your accounts.
